# I Built a Cutting Board

I just finished my first woodworking project - a large maple cutting board. If you clicked into this article looking for a smart metaphor or deep dive into my primal need to work with wood in the exhausting age of AI and tech overabundance, then _CLICK AGAIN_! My intent here is simply to share how I built the board and some of the beginner lessons I learned along the way.

## Strategy

I used Claude Opus 5.5 as my resident woodworking expert and YouTube for tool and skill demos, especially where safety was concerned. Semi-unfortunately, this was more than enough to help me complete the project without finding a human mentor or community to build with.

## Workspace, Materials, and Tools

### Workspace

There's an old (but fantastic) workbench in the unfinished basement of the house we're currently renting. I de-cobwebbed the chunk of the basement it lives in and medium-to-deep cleaned it, revitalizing the perfect space to cut, glue, and sand!

![fig. 1 — the workbench](/articles/2026-10-18/workbench.jpg "211")

### Materials

I chose maple for the board, because it felt nice and Canadian and because I learned it's dense and durable enough to endure years of knife cuts. Alternatives I considered but ended up skipping for now were walnut and cherry.

Bought Gorilla Wood Glue since it's food safe and came in a small enough bottle to not need to invest in 50 years of glue. Went with Butcher's Block mineral oil to finish the board with a nice look and feel and increase its lifespan.

I also bought a pine 2x4 board for practice cuts, and some insulation foam board for cutting directly on my bench without requiring the wood to overhang off its front edge.

### Tools

I already own a DeWalt cordless drill, and learned that it's important to **buy tools of the same battery platform** to avoid spending an arm and a leg on batteries. So I bought a DeWalt cordless circular saw for my crosscuts (which I learned means cutting across the grain - i.e. cutting a long board into shorter slats of the same width). I also bought a corded 5" random orbital sander for sanding. I went with Ryobi for this one since it was cheaper, had great reviews, and didn't require a battery. To go with the sander, I got four grits of sandpaper pads in a starter multipack (80, 120, 180, and 220 grit).

Hand tools were massively useful too. I bought a speed square for marking and guiding the crosscuts. I also acquired four 24-inch bar clamps which were super useful for the glue-up and for holding my wood still when cutting and sanding.

I already had a pencil, microfiber cloths, foam brushes, and a tape measure at home, but those too became best friends of mine throughout the project.

Oh, and as they say, safety last! Safety glasses, an N95 mask, and ear protection were key!

## Shopping

Conveniently, I was able to buy everything at Home Depot (woohoo!). For future projects, I'll probably find a more specialized location for wood and mineral oil since stock and variety were limited for both.

## Building the Board

### Cutting

I decided to aim for a board size of approximately **17.5 x 12 x 3/4 in**. This equated to seven 12-inch strips of my 2.5-inch wide boards glued side to side. However, I knew my first time crosscutting perfect foot-long boards would be rough, so I decided to cut 13-inch boards (knowing they'd be uneven) and cut them straight after the glue-up.

![fig. 2 — the plan](/articles/2026-10-18/plan.png "323")

YouTube was massively helpful for learning how to install the blade and do my practice cuts on pine 2x4s without cutting my fingers off. The speed square made cutting perfectly straightforward, both figuratively and literally. Some important lessons I learned:

- **Always** take the battery out when not using the saw
- Clamp the speed square to the wood if you can't get a good grip
- Line up both the blade marker and the blade before cutting, for two points of reference
- Allow the saw to reach full rotational speed before cutting
- Support both sides of the board you're cutting, to avoid tear-outs (imperfections) on the boards' edges as they fall
- Always wear your glasses - chips and dust can shoot up any time!

### Glue-Up

I laid down a small tarp and arranged my 7 boards in a pattern I thought was visually appealing, and drew a large triangle on top across the boards so I could easily remember the right front-face and board order. Without applying any glue, I dry fit my boards together in the clamps, to learn how to use the clamps and approximate them to the right size.

![fig. 3 — dry fit](/articles/2026-10-18/dry-fit.jpg "205")

After unclamping, I squeezed a healthy line of food-safe Gorilla Wood Glue on one face of each joint, then painted them uniformly across each plank using a foam brush. Once all the glue was painted on, I arranged all pieces together and set the clamps, aligning all the bottoms of the planks flush while leaving the top side misaligned (since each board was a slightly different length from the rough cuts). I could tell the glue amount was good because a small amount oozed out of each joint.

About 45 minutes into drying in the clamps, I used a metal putty knife to scrape any large jelly-textured glue beads off the boards since I read that those can be quite hard to sand. Thin and flat glue marks were easy to sand later. 24 hours later, I had a single solid structure!

### Clean-up Cut

As expected, three of the board's edges were fairly aligned (with some small bumps). But the top edge was a mess, as planned. I used a spare pine 2x4 as a straight edge paired with the circular saw to trim a half-inch off the messy edge of the board, making it perfectly straight and trimming the whole board to about 12.5".

![fig. 4 — clean-up cut](/articles/2026-10-18/clean-up-cut.jpg "188")

## Finishing the Board

### Sanding

For a beginner woodworker like me, this is where most of the magic happened. At this point, you could still tell both by sight and touch that these were 7 boards glued together. The goal was to keep the visual segmentation of the boards' patterns, but use multiple grits of sanding to make the board feel like one super-smooth uniform surface on all faces.

80 grit did most of the work for this project. I was absolutely amazed by how much material it took off and how smooth it left the board feeling on the glued joints, even before the higher grits of sandpaper were applied.

Something I learned is that higher grits don't take off more. Each level of grit removes less material and removes the smaller chips that the last grit left behind. As I stepped up through 80, 120, 180, and 220, I was blown away by how the board kept getting smoother and smoother to touch. I used the random orbital sander to do the front and back faces of the board, and for the thin edges, I wrapped a sandpaper pad around a small piece of scrap wood to use as a flat hand-held sanding surface.

An awesome trick I learned was to scribble all over the board with pencil before each grit level. Once all pencil was gone, it was clear that I had at least covered the entire board once. After that (for 80 grit), I would feel for any high or uneven spots and spend more time focused there. 120, 180, and 220 were all significantly quicker as there was less material to remove.

I noticed the corners and edges of the board were a bit sharp, so I sanded them by hand into a slightly softened corner that still had a sharp look.

![fig. 5 — sanded](/articles/2026-10-18/sanded.jpg "198")

At this point, the board was feeling buttery, and really started to look like a professional product.

### Raising the Grain & Oil

I used a spray bottle on the board to raise the grain, and did one more quick round of 220 grit to remove the fuzz. After wiping any remaining dried fuzz with a microfiber towel, I used a separate towel to rub Butcher's Block mineral oil on all faces of the board. I did this four times total, waiting at least an hour or two between each coat. This really brought out the grain of the maple and darkened the wood by a few shades. It also keeps the wood healthier, and can be reapplied to rejuvenate the board.

## What's Next?

Overall, I was thrilled with the final look and feel of the board, and was surprised by the quality I was able to produce as a beginner with a few tools.
