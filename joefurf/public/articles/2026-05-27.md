# Five Lessons from Year One at Stripe

April 21st marked my first full year at Stripe. Incredible people, relentless learning, and somewhere along the way, writing code by hand became a dead art. Here are five things I've learned so far, *in no particular order.*

### Users First

Stripe employees and systems embody the company's first operating principle: **Users first**. Don't invent your users' identities or needs. Regardless of whether you're a startup founder or a core infrastructure guru, build what your users need, and align your priorities with theirs. Imagined value is not real value, so your time is best spent shipping things that people want.

Build urgently, but not at the cost of poor design and modeling. Deliver real value to your users as fast as you can without taking shortcuts. The "long term perfect system" almost always isn't the one your users need today, and you can almost always get there iteratively. I repeat. Do not "one shot" the perfect thing. Get there iteratively over time, as real user need arises.

For platform and infra work, paying customers are not the only users that matter. Make code more ergonomic and readable for other engineers and agents. Take feedback seriously and act on it quickly. Today's LLMs make refactors and tech debt cleanups effectively free. Tech debt can be tackled iteratively far more often than people think. You likely don't need the "full rewrite" or the "great restructure".

Users do more than pay the bills. They deliver the sense of real impact most of us are after. Lean into that.

### Operate

Building without operating is just prototyping. The merge is not the finish line. Passing tests and local demos aren't "shipping". Infrastructure breaks. Test coverage misses. Observability is how you find what went wrong and where, so you can fix it.

I didn't appreciate any of this before this year. Operating a system in production means knowing which dashboard tells you the truth, which logs to use when the dashboard isn't enough, and which team to pull in when you don't own the problematic system. It means writing the cleanup CRON jobs and invariant checks that catch bad data state before it compounds. None of this is glamorous, and none of it shows up in the release notes, but it's most of what keeps real systems alive. Anyone can vibe-code a demo. Real systems have to scale and keep working at 3am when shit hits the fan.

### Parallelize

Focusing on a single coding task for a multi-hour session is obsolete. You're a manager of agents now. Plan carefully, so that your agents have enough clarity and scope to act over a long period of time with autonomy. While they are acting, you can craft more plans, write design docs, or review agent-written diffs. You can also grab a coffee, call your mom, or take a washroom break. Every minute you offload to a busy agent with clear guardrails is a minute you earn back to queue up or create more parallel professional and personal value.

Plan deeply. Push back often. Agents are not always right, and blindly accepting every agent decision doesn't multiply your value – it multiplies reviewer friction and tech debt. Invest time in systems that increase your parallelization: subagents, automatic code review, skill suites, notifications for attention switching.

### Just Fix It

Some things don't require planning, funding, or discussion. If there's a solution that would make your team's life better, just implement it. Code cleanups, team processes, small tools that nobody owns but everyone would benefit from. Earlier this year I went on a side-quest and built a Slack bot for my team that answers incoming requests and questions before our on-call has time to.

This kind of work doesn't show up in your performance review the way features do, but your teammates and your manager notice. Over time it becomes your reputation: the person who makes things better without being asked. And the mindset is contagious. Be that person, and the people around you start being that person too.

### DaaS

Discomfort as a Signal. The thread you're scared to participate in because you don't know enough is the one you should reply to. The person you're worried will destroy your design is exactly the person who should review it. The AI tool everyone's been talking about that you've been quietly justifying not using is the one you should spend a weekend learning. The project you're putting off because it's too complex is the project that will teach you what you've been avoiding learning.

Every dip in comfort and confidence is like a tiny sensor in your brain signalling your next step in your career and technical development. Let these signals shape your day-to-day priorities. Treat them as fuel, not fear.

## Below the Cutline
A few more that didn't quite make the top five:

- Respect your own time and other people's to the minute.
- Protect long blocks of uninterrupted focus, every day.
- Don't be verbose. Saying more with less is a skill, and people notice.
- Be an active participant in meetings. Ask the stupid questions. Everyone is constantly learning, including the people who look like they're not.
- Be available. The fact that you make time to mentor someone matters more than what you say once you do.
